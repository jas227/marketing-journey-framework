# The Marketing Journey Framework

Use the Marketing Journey Framework to model how a person moves from experiencing a problem or desire to choosing a product solution. Its components are **The 18 Atomic Units of Marketing**. Treat strategy as a connected system of hypotheses and decisions rather than eighteen independent answers.

## Orient the user compactly

Do not begin a company application with a long framework explanation unless the user requests teaching or documentation.

For an interactive map, place one concise orientation line beneath the title. State that the map contains researched possibilities across the 18 Atomic Units, that a journey isolates one coherent path, and that hovering or tapping reveals definitions and interpretation.

Attach concise hover or tap descriptions to:

- the supergroups **Marketing Context**, **Marketing Activities**, and **Offer**;
- the groups **Human Context**, **Decision Environment**, **Market Engagement**, **Brand World**, **Value Proposition**, and **Pricing and Proof**;
- all 18 atomic-unit headings; and
- every company-specific possibility.

Describe framework levels generically and describe individual possibilities with company-specific evidence or strategic interpretation.

## The 18 Atomic Units

Always use these groups, units, and this order.

### Marketing Context

#### 1. Human Context

1. **ICPs/Personas:** The people or organizations being prioritized. Use useful levels of specificity and distinguish account, buyer, user, champion, and blocker when relevant.
2. **Category Entry Moments:** Concrete events, situations, or changes that move a problem or desire from latent to active consideration and cause someone to enter a buying category.
3. **Psychological States:** How the persona experiences and interprets the entry moment, including emotions, motivations, beliefs, identity tensions, perceived risks, and readiness to act. Describe the state before marketing or the product creates its intended effect.

#### 2. Decision Environment

4. **Buying Categories/Industries:** The broad mental, budgetary, or industry universe in which competitors and alternatives live. Phrase these as recognizable arenas of choice rather than product descriptions.
5. **Competitors/Alternatives:** Every meaningful response within the selected category, including direct competitors, substitutes, DIY, hiring someone, delaying, and doing nothing.
6. **Influence Sources:** The people, peers, communities, institutions, media, experts, internal stakeholders, and algorithms that shape understanding and decisions.

### Marketing Activities

#### 3. Market Engagement

7. **Channels:** The specific contexts where marketing moments happen, including search, named social platforms, communities, referrals, sales calls, events, marketplaces, physical locations, publications, and the product experience.
8. **Informational Content:** Media whose primary job is to help the audience understand the problem, category, alternatives, surrounding decision, or life situation without centering the company's own products or services. Include category education, timely changes, how-to guidance, decision criteria, research, checklists, and calculators. Exclude product pages, company-specific service explainers, feature demonstrations, testimonials, and case studies.
9. **Experiential Content:** Media, artifacts, interactions, or activations whose primary job is to create feeling, identification, aspiration, enjoyment, participation, belonging, or memorability. Include customer films, entertainment, games, interactive experiences, challenges, events, humor, and cultural collaborations.

#### 4. Brand World

10. **Stories/Narratives:** The larger worldview and narratives: why the business exists, what it believes, what has changed, and what future it wants to help create.
11. **Aesthetic Directions:** The overall vibes and cultural, emotional, identity, or status signals the brand could embody. Begin with experiential territories before specifying colors, fonts, or executions.
12. **Language Directions:** How the brand should speak, including tone, vocabulary, cadence, point of view, rhetorical posture, and verbal conventions. Define a recognizable voice territory that works across channels.

### Offer

#### 5. Value Proposition

13. **Benefits:** The functional, economic, emotional, social, and identity-level transformations offered.
14. **Features:** The concrete capabilities, mechanisms, services, experiences, and components producing those benefits.
15. **Purchase Categories:** Customer-recognizable families of products, services, plans, or offers available from the brand. Keep these distinct from the external Buying Categories/Industries.

#### 6. Pricing and Proof

16. **Pricing Options/Structures:** Price level, pricing metric, packaging, commitment, terms, anchoring, financing, discounting, guarantees, and perceived risk.
17. **Quantitative Social Proof:** Numerical evidence supporting a claim, including adoption, customer counts, savings, revenue, performance, ratings, retention, repeat behavior, distribution, or market tenure. Tie every metric to the claim it supports.
18. **Qualitative Social Proof:** Human, institutional, or observational evidence supporting a claim, including testimonials, case studies, customer examples, recognizable customers, retailer validation, endorsements, press, reviews, referrals, and community participation.

Keep these boundaries clear:

- Buying Categories/Industries define the universe in which competitors and alternatives live.
- Competitors/Alternatives are the ways the buyer could satisfy the need in that universe.
- Category Entry Moments identify what changed; Psychological States identify how the person experiences that change.
- Channels are where marketing moments occur.
- Informational Content helps the audience understand a problem, category, alternatives, or decision without making the company's offering the subject.
- Experiential Content creates a felt or participatory encounter.
- Classify an execution by its primary strategic job. A results-focused customer video is usually Qualitative Social Proof; an identification-focused customer film can be Experiential Content.
- Stories/Narratives supply meaning; Aesthetic Directions give it experiential form; Language Directions govern verbal expression.
- Benefits describe the transformation; Features describe the mechanism; Purchase Categories organize what can be bought.
- Pricing defines the exchange; Quantitative and Qualitative Social Proof reduce risk with different forms of evidence.

## Model important statements explicitly

Classify important statements as:

- **Market hypothesis:** A belief about external reality that can be investigated.
- **Strategic choice:** A direction the business controls and may revise.
- **Constraint:** A condition currently limiting available choices.
- **Commitment:** A principle or choice maintained independently of optimization.

When useful, record evidence, confidence, dependencies, consequence if wrong, and status. Use statuses such as `untested`, `testing`, `supported`, `contradicted`, and `held constant`.

A held constant is an explicit choice rather than a claim about market reality. Its market consequences remain empirical.

## Choose the working mode

Infer the mode and combine modes when useful:

- **Explore:** Generate three to five distinct possibilities for every unit, or exactly the number requested. Present them as candidate hypotheses.
- **Configure:** Select a coherent leading answer for each unit and explain exclusions and tradeoffs.
- **Audit:** Map an existing strategy, then identify missing assumptions, contradictions, unsupported claims, and accidental constants.
- **Validate:** Turn consequential uncertainties into a prioritized hypothesis register and practical tests.
- **Compare:** Build two or three internally coherent configurations when the business could plausibly pursue different personas, categories, narratives, or offers.

If the request is open-ended, begin with Explore and finish with a provisional Configure synthesis.

## Establish the evidence base

For an existing business, research current first-party information and relevant customer or market evidence. Use current web research when facts may have changed and cite factual claims. For an early idea, work from the user's description and label assumptions rather than inventing market facts.

Separate:

- known facts supplied by the user or supported by sources;
- reasonable inferences;
- candidate hypotheses;
- strategic choices or commitments fixed by the user.

Ask a question only when the missing answer would materially alter the work and cannot be handled with a clearly stated assumption.

## Test coherence

After populating the units, test whether:

- the persona plausibly experiences the entry moment, enters the proposed psychological state, and recognizes the buying category;
- the competitors and alternatives reflect how that person could actually respond;
- influence sources shape the decision and the channels can reach those pathways;
- informational content addresses questions created by the entry moment, category, alternatives, and decision while remaining useful without centering the offer;
- experiential content fits the psychological state and brand world;
- stories, aesthetics, and language create one recognizable brand world;
- the benefits matter, the features can produce them, and the purchase categories make the offer navigable;
- pricing fits value, alternatives, risk, buying process, and ability to pay; and
- quantitative and qualitative proof support the claims that matter at purchase.

Call out contradictions directly. A complete inventory of possibilities does not establish a coherent strategy.

## Synthesize the strategy

Produce a concise strategy spine showing how one selected path connects the persona, entry moment, psychological state, buying category, alternatives, influences, channels, informational and experiential content, brand world, benefits, features, purchase category, pricing, and proof.

Use natural prose rather than forcing all 18 units into one sentence.

## Create three Customer Marketing Journeys

Whenever applying the framework to an existing company, include exactly three distinct Customer Marketing Journeys unless the user requests a different number or asks for the map only.

Each journey must:

- follow one coherent customer from an activated situation through consideration, discovery, brand interpretation, purchase, and outcome;
- naturally incorporate all 18 atomic units without reading like a checklist;
- choose only the possibilities belonging to that journey, usually one from each unit;
- differ meaningfully in persona, entry moment, buying category, or route to market;
- distinguish sourced company facts from inferred behavior and strategic hypotheses; and
- explain how benefits, features, purchase category, pricing, and proof produce conversion.

Keep each story compact. After each story, retain an explicit or machine-readable list of the exact option IDs it touches.

## Visualize journeys through the possibility map

For every interactive company map or Customer Marketing Journey visualization, use the canonical visual system in [references/visual-language.md](references/visual-language.md). Read that reference before generating the visualization. Treat it as a required rendering contract: company research and option content change, while the map's visual language, hierarchy, color sequence, controls, and interaction model remain consistent.

Do not substitute a network graph, force-directed canvas, relationship-line diagram, vertical dashboard, table-first layout, or company-branded redesign unless the user explicitly requests a different visualization. The default must look and behave like the canonical six-column Marketing Journey Map.

When delivering the map:

1. Show the complete map first in an unhighlighted **All Possibilities** state.
2. Add controls for **Journey 1**, **Journey 2**, **Journey 3**, and **All Possibilities**.
3. When a journey is selected, show its story and softly highlight only the option nodes listed in its `touches` array. Keep other possibilities visible but secondary.
4. Preserve the left-to-right six-stage flow. Use subtle containers around each atomic unit. Do not add relationship lines unless requested.
5. Add compact hover or tap explanations for supergroups, groups, atomic-unit headings, and individual possibilities.
6. Do not use a permanent selected-item summary pill above the journeys.
7. Prevent text and columns from overlapping. Preserve the conceptual flow with horizontal scrolling at narrow widths when necessary.
8. Do not preselect a journey on initial load.
9. Keep the canonical neutral visual language: white canvas, restrained dark typography, muted secondary copy, light-gray rounded atomic-unit cards, six slim group-color bars, short matching color rules beside each possibility, and rounded journey controls.
10. Keep the title, orientation line, journey controls, optional journey story panel, supergroup headers, and six-column map in that exact vertical order.

Use journey records containing `title`, `story`, and `touches`. Validate that every referenced option ID exists before presenting the map.

## Prioritize validation

Prioritize hypotheses by:

1. consequence if wrong;
2. current uncertainty;
3. number of downstream units depending on the hypothesis; and
4. cost and speed of learning.

For every proposed test, specify the hypothesis, evidence sought, method, and result that would change the strategy. Prefer observable behavior over stated preference when practical.

## Output conventions

- Call the methodology **The Marketing Journey Framework** and its components **The 18 Atomic Units of Marketing**.
- Title company applications **[Company] Marketing Journey Map**.
- Call coherent paths **Customer Marketing Journeys**.
- Use the six groups and canonical unit order.
- Use the exact capitalized atomic-unit headings without adding “Possible.” The contents remain hypotheses and possibilities.
- Keep possibilities distinct rather than rephrasing one idea several ways.
- Distinguish facts, hypotheses, choices, constraints, and commitments.
- Avoid presenting market fit or customer demand as established without evidence.
- For company maps, include the three Customer Marketing Journeys and interactive highlighting behavior.
- In a working canvas, give each selected unit a current hypothesis, type, evidence, confidence, and next test.
